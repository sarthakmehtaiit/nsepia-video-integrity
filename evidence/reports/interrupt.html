5 · How conclusions are drawn & limitations

  • Transfer issues are decided only by size + SHA-256 (client vs. server). A verified transfer means the bytes are identical; it says nothing about the recording.
  • file layer: decoder/demuxer errors and declared-vs-decoded counts → damage or encoding fault in the file itself.
  • source layer: reference counter burned into each picture → proves a capture frame is missing/duplicated.
  • timing layer: PTS gaps/repeats → frames never written at capture/encode time.
  • content layer: picture similarity → suggestive only.
  • A byte-perfect transfer (matching SHA-256) proves only that the backend holds exactly the bytes the client sent. It cannot prove that the recording itself has no dropped frames.
  • Without a reference counter, missing frames are inferred from timestamp gaps. If a capture pipeline drops frames and then re-stamps the remaining frames at a regular rate, the drop is invisible to timestamps and can only be suspected from abrupt picture changes.
  • Frozen (identical) consecutive pictures may be a capture freeze or a genuinely static scene; content analysis alone cannot tell them apart.
  • Resampling to 30 FPS intentionally repeats frames (sources < 30 FPS) or omits them (sources > 30 FPS); those are reported as conversion effects, not anomalies.
  • Decoder error concealment can hide small corruptions: a damaged slice may decode without an error but with visual artefacts that no metric here flags.
  • Abrupt-change detection uses a statistical threshold; scene cuts in real footage produce false positives.
